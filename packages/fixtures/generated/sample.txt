# Lighthouse Notes

A lighthouse is a promise made of stone and glass. It tells anyone listening on the water that the land has been measured, and that somebody expects them to arrive. These notes describe one tower on a cold coast, the lamp at the top of it, and the people whose whole job was to keep that lamp burning.

## The First Light

The first tower was little more than a stone hut with a fire on its roof. It burned driftwood and pitch, and it burned badly. On a clear night a sailor could see the glow from a mile away; in fog it showed nothing at all. The lighthouse keepers complained that the fire ate more fuel than it gave light, and they were right.

Half a century later the hut had become a proper tower of cut stone, forty metres of it, standing on a headland that the old charts called the widow's shelf. Its lamp was a ring of oil burners behind a lens of concentric glass, and the lens did what a thousand bonfires could not: it gathered the light and threw it out again in a narrow beam that reached the horizon.

The tower was painted in broad horizontal bands, so that a sailor could tell one lighthouse from another by day as well as by night. The bands were not decoration. They were a name, and a name is cheaper to read than a bearing.

## The Keeper's Round

A keeper's day was a round of small duties, each one timed to the weather rather than to the clock.

- Trim the wicks and polish the lens before dusk, because soot on glass costs more light than a weak flame.
- Wind the clockwork that turns the lamp, because a rotation that has stopped reads as a light that is not there.
- Write the log at midnight and again at dawn, because a night that is not recorded is a night that is not trusted.

The log was kept in pencil on paper that the damp turned soft, and it recorded what mattered: the hours of darkness, the direction of the wind, the ships that passed, and the long nights when the fog held so hard that the horn spoke instead of the lamp.

> A light that is not tended is only a rumour, and a rumour will not hold a ship off a rock.

Keepers were expected to stay awake, but the sea does not care what is expected of a man. Men fell asleep beside the lamp, and the log says so, written up the next morning in a hand that is careful and ashamed. The tower's answer was mechanical: a weight, a chain, and a clockwork turn that could not be argued out of its duty.

Whatever the weather was doing at night, the lighthouse was lit from dusk to dawn. A keeper who could not light it wrote down why, in the plain words the service asked for, and the service read those words every winter.

## The Modern Lamp

The oil burners went out a long time ago. Electricity came, then automation, and the last resident keeper left in the spring of a year that the log does not name. The lamp stayed behind. Its great lens was taken to a museum, where it sits behind glass and gathers nothing but the curiosity of visitors.

What remains is a smaller lamp in a smaller tower: a steel lattice with a sealed box at the top, which the charts still mark as a lighthouse. It flashes four times in twenty seconds, and that pattern is printed on every chart of this coast. The pattern is the whole point. A light without a pattern is a light without a name, and a sailor who cannot name a light will not trust it.

The tower is unmanned now, and the round of duties belongs to a contractor who drives out twice a year in a van. He changes the batteries, checks the seals, and writes his initials in a book that is no longer called a log. He does not stay the night. Nobody does.

1. The original lamp burned whale oil, and the keeper trimmed its wick every evening with a pair of brass scissors.
